## Short blurb (store-listing length)

> *What the Ground Remembers* is a quiet, unsettling exploration game about a stretch of the Norwegian coast that exists in two time periods at once — and the things its people believe, half-remember, or won't quite say about what connects them.

## Longer description

The Norwegian coast of Lofoten, Vesterålen, and Salten looks like an ordinary place — fishing villages, farmland, a tidal current strong enough to carve the seabed. Walk it in one direction and it's the present day. Walk it in the other, and it's the Viking Age, the same ground centuries earlier.

*What the Ground Remembers* is an atmospheric, exploration-first game about moving between those two moments in the same place — not through a machine or a spell, just a step sideways the player can take at will — and piecing together what actually connects them. Nobody hands you a citation. A farmer mentions a rise in her field her tractor always jumps at. A homeowner doesn't like to think about what was dug up under his house. A trader, centuries earlier, touches the mast before rounding a point on the water and won't say why, exactly, except that the ones who didn't never came back to explain it. The connections are real and researched — grounded in actual archaeology and history from the region — but the game never tells you they're true. It only shows you what people believe.

There's no combat here, and no fantasy magic that works. What tension exists comes from mood, uncertainty, and the sense that a place remembers something it isn't telling you — closer to *What Remains of Edith Finch*, *Old Man's Journey*, or *Outer Wilds* than to anything sword-and-shield. It's built for players willing to sit with an unresolved feeling rather than have it explained away.

## Feature bullets

- **Move between two time periods on the same ground.** A tap, click, or keypress switches, with a brief fade, between a recognizable modern Norwegian setting and the same landscape in the Viking Age — no loading screen, no explained time-travel mechanism.
- **Connections delivered as belief, not fact.** What ties the two eras together comes out in conversation, rumor, and things people leave half-said — never a certainty rating or a source citation.
- **Three locations grounded in real Nordland history and archaeology**, in the Lofoten/Vesterålen/Salten region — fictionalized, not literal recreations, but built from real research rather than invented lore.
- **Exploration over combat.** No fighting system; tension comes from atmosphere, pacing, and what's left unsaid, not danger to the player character.
- **Built for the browser.** Play directly at the game's URL — no install.

## Self-review against `docs/research/authenticity_and_sensitive_topics.md`

- **No romanticizing of violence, plunder, or slavery**: none of the copy above frames raiding/conquest as the game's subject — it's explicitly framed as exploration/atmosphere over combat, consistent with §2.1-2.3's guidance.
- **Sámi-Norse content is not mentioned or claimed anywhere in this copy.** This is deliberate, not an oversight: per issue #26's review, no Sámi-related content currently exists in the game (the Saltstraumen quest is Norse-only). Marketing copy claiming Sámi representation that isn't actually in the game would both overpromise an unbuilt feature (this issue's own criterion) and reduce real Sámi history to a marketing bullet point without the content to back it — exactly what §2.7 warns against. If/when Sámi-Norse content is actually built (subject to §2.7's full guidance, including the recommendation to involve a Sámi institution), this copy should be revisited to represent it accurately, not before.
- **No ethnic-purity/genetic-continuity framing**: the copy describes connection through "culture, language, place, and institutions" language (implicitly, via "what connects them" framed as belief/research), not blood/ethnic continuity — consistent with §2.6.
- **Doesn't overclaim certainty**: "grounded in actual archaeology and history... but the game never tells you they're true" mirrors the project's actual design stance rather than marketing the history as settled fact.
